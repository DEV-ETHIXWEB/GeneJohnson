Winter storms are exactly when you need your generator to work without a hitch — and exactly when a poorly maintained one tends to let you down. A little prep now saves you from troubleshooting in the dark during an outage.

## Inspect and clean

Start with a general inspection. Replace the air filter if it's clogged, clean the spark plugs, and confirm the electrode gap matches manufacturer specs. Check the fuel system for clogs, leaks, or other shenanigans, and drain any stagnant fuel left in the tank or carburetor bowl.

## Winterize the fuel system

Fuel that's sat for more than a month should be discarded. Replace the fuel filter and treat fresh fuel with a stabilizer. For carbureted models, run the generator for a few minutes until the stabilized fuel cycles all the way into the carburetor, then drain the bowl immediately afterward. Store any extra fuel in approved, airtight containers.

## Take care of the battery

Check the battery's charge using the gauge window and hook up a trickle charger if it's running low. Clean corroded terminals with a brush and a diluted baking soda solution. If the generator will sit unused for months, consider removing the battery and using a battery tender to keep it healthy through the off-season.

## Store it properly

Keep the generator in a dry, ventilated space like a shed or garage, and use a weatherproof cover once it's fully cooled. Set out rodent repellent, since generators sitting idle are a common target for nesting critters.

## Cold-weather starting tips

Switch to a lighter oil viscosity — 5W-30 or 10W-30 — for winter operation. Prime the oil before startup, warm the engine gradually, and let it idle 5–10 minutes before pushing it to full load.

## Keep it exercised

Run the generator 15–30 minutes every month, even when you don't need it, and change the oil on the manufacturer's recommended schedule.
