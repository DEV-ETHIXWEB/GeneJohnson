Knob-and-tube wiring shows up constantly in Puget Sound homes built before the 1950s, and it tends to alarm homeowners the moment an inspector or contractor mentions it. The honest answer is more nuanced than "dangerous" or "fine."

## What knob-and-tube actually is

It's an early wiring method using ceramic knobs and tubes to run individual insulated conductors through wall cavities and joists, without a grounding conductor and without the sheathing modern cable has. On its own, undisturbed and unmodified, it isn't inherently a fire hazard.

## Where the real risk comes from

- **No ground wire** - meaning many modern appliances and surge/GFCI protections can't function as designed
- **Insulation contact** - knob-and-tube was designed to dissipate heat into open air; modern blown-in attic insulation covering it traps heat and is a genuine fire risk
- **Decades of amateur splices and additions** - the original wiring is often fine, but additions made by previous owners over the decades frequently aren't
- **Brittle, degraded insulation** - the rubberized cloth insulation becomes brittle with age and can crack or crumble

## Why insurance and lenders care

Many insurers either decline to cover homes with active knob-and-tube wiring or require an inspection and rewiring commitment. The same is often true for mortgage lenders and refinancing.

## What we actually recommend

Rather than a blanket "rip it all out," we start with an inspection to see the wiring's actual condition, what's been added to it over the years, and whether it's in contact with insulation. From there, a full or partial [rewire](/electrical-wiring-rewiring/) is usually the right call for a home you plan to keep long-term, insure normally, and eventually sell without an asterisk on the inspection report.
