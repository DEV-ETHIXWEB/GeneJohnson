Backflow is one of those plumbing problems most homeowners never think about until something goes wrong — and by then, the issue is a health hazard, not just an inconvenience. Understanding the devices that prevent it is worth a few minutes of your time.

## What Backflow Actually Is

Backflow happens when water flows in the reverse direction through your plumbing system, potentially allowing contaminated water to mix with your clean water supply. It's most likely to occur when there's a sudden drop in water pressure — a water main break or heavy simultaneous demand, for example — that pulls water backward through a connection that should only flow one way.

## Air Gaps

An air gap is the simplest form of backflow prevention: an open, upright space between a water outlet and the flood level of a fixture. It's a purely physical barrier — no moving parts, nothing to fail — that prevents drain water from ever flowing back into the clean supply.

## Pressure Vacuum Breakers (PVBs)

PVBs use a control valve that opens and closes based on water pressure. If pressure drops below air pressure — a common trigger for backflow — the valve closes to keep non-drinking water, most often from an irrigation system, from siphoning back into the clean supply.

## Atmospheric Vacuum Breakers (AVBs)

AVBs are a more affordable option built around a hood that opens to let air into the system during a back-siphonage event. A poppet valve operates the mechanism, and while they're simple and effective, they typically aren't rated for continuous pressure applications the way a PVB is.

## Choosing the Right Device

The right backflow preventer for your home depends on your specific plumbing layout and how your sewer line is configured. In some cases, assembly preventers or reduced-pressure zone devices — which use a closing mechanism to block backflow through the pipeline — are the more appropriate choice, particularly for irrigation systems or commercial connections.

## Check Before You Install

Many jurisdictions, including areas around Seattle, require permits or specific devices for backflow prevention, so it's worth checking with your local building authority before installation.
