Every Seattle household deals with a clogged sink sooner or later. Food particles, grease, hair, and soap scum build up inside drain pipes until water simply stops going down. Here's how the pros actually clear a stubborn clog — and when it's worth calling in help.

## Disjointing the Drain Pipes

One of the most direct approaches is to open the pipe joints under the sink, at the trap and the main drain line. With the pipe apart, a flexible rod can be worked down the line repeatedly to physically dislodge the blockage before everything gets reassembled. It's messy but effective for clogs sitting close to the fixture.

## The Drain Auger

For clogs further down the line, plumbers reach for a drain auger — a long, flexible metal cable with a cone-shaped tip designed to navigate bends in the pipe and hook onto debris. The crank is turned clockwise until the auger makes contact with the obstruction, and then water is flushed through forcefully to carry the loosened material the rest of the way out.

## Plungers and Chemical Treatments

A plunger works by creating vacuum pressure that can dislodge softer clogs on its own. For grease and oils that have hardened inside the pipe, chemical treatments are sometimes used, but they need to be handled carefully — overly aggressive chemicals like sodium hydroxide or sulfuric acid can damage pipes if used incorrectly, so this is one area where professional judgment matters.

## When to Call a Plumber

If a clog keeps coming back, resists a plunger and store-bought drain cleaner, or is affecting more than one fixture at once, that's usually a sign of a bigger problem in your [drains](/drains/) beyond the sink itself. Persistent or recurring clogs are also a good candidate for [hydrojetting](/hydrojetting/), which clears buildup far more thoroughly than a snake alone.
