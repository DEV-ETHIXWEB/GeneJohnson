Pouring grease down the sink feels harmless in the moment — it's liquid, after all. But grease cools and hardens quickly once it hits the cooler pipes in your walls, and that's where the trouble starts.

## What Grease Actually Does to Your Pipes

**Clogged drains.** Solidified grease coats the inside of pipes and traps food particles and hair as they pass through, building into a stubborn clog. Slow drainage and gurgling sounds are usually the first signs.

**Sewer backups.** Enough grease buildup can restrict flow badly enough to cause raw sewage to back up into the home — a genuine health hazard from the bacteria and pathogens involved, not just an inconvenience.

**Expensive repairs.** Clearing a grease clog with a professional hydro-jetter or rooter can easily run into the hundreds or even thousands of dollars, and in severe cases the affected section of pipe may need to be replaced entirely.

## How to Dispose of Grease the Right Way

- Let it cool and solidify, then scrape it into the trash.
- Pour it into a sealed container first if it's still liquid, to avoid spills.
- Check for a local recycling program — Seattle offers curbside grease collection.
- Strained, cooled grease can also be refrigerated and reused in cooking for up to six months.

## If You've Already Got a Clog

A mix of hot water and dish soap, or baking soda followed by vinegar, can help with a mild grease clog. If that doesn't clear it, or if you notice a foul odor coming from the drain, it's time to call a professional rather than keep pouring things down the drain and hoping.

## Prevention Going Forward

Schedule an annual professional [drains](/drains/) cleaning, use an enzyme-based drain cleaner between visits, and make sure everyone in the household knows grease belongs in the trash, not the sink.
