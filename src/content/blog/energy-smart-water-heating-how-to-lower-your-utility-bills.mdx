Water heating quietly accounts for about 20 percent of the average household's energy use. That makes it one of the biggest opportunities for lowering your utility bill — through both equipment choices and a few everyday habits.

## Storage Tanks vs. Tankless

Traditional storage tank water heaters keep a full tank of water hot around the clock, which means a steady stream of standby energy loss even when no one's using hot water. [Tankless water heaters](/tankless-water-heaters/) take a different approach, heating water only on demand, which cuts out that wasted energy entirely while still delivering consistent hot water.

## Daily Habits That Add Up

A few small changes make a real difference over a billing cycle: shortening showers by even two minutes, washing laundry in cold water when possible, fixing leaks as soon as you notice them, running the dishwasher and washing machine only with full loads, and installing [low-flow fixtures](/toilet-repair-installation/) to reduce overall hot water demand.

## Why Tankless Makes Sense for Older Homes

Beyond the energy savings, tankless systems have a smaller physical footprint than a bulky storage tank — a real advantage in older Seattle-area homes where utility space is tight. They avoid wasting energy keeping unused water hot, which is exactly the inefficiency that drives up bills with an aging storage tank system.

## Keeping Your System Efficient

Whichever type of [water heater](/water-heaters/) you have, a bit of regular maintenance keeps it running efficiently: flush sediment buildup periodically (a bigger concern with the mineral content in local water), inspect the anode rod for corrosion, keep the thermostat set around 120°F, and test the pressure relief valve annually.

## Choosing the Right System

The right water heater for your home depends on household size, how many people need hot water at once, the unit's Energy Factor rating, your available fuel source, and how much installation space you have to work with.

## Knowing When to Replace

Storage tank water heaters typically last 8 to 12 years, while tankless systems can run 15 to 20 years or more. Rust-colored water, unusual noises, inconsistent temperatures, and a creeping utility bill are all signs it may be time to talk to a professional about a replacement.
