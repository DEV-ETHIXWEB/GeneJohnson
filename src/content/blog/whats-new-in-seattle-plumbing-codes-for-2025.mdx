Plumbing codes rarely make headlines, but they directly affect what's allowed in your home and what a contractor can legally install. Seattle's 2025 updates bring several meaningful changes worth knowing about, whether you're planning a renovation or just want to understand what's required going forward.

## Water conservation gets stricter

Low-flow toilets and faucets are now the default expectation for both residential and commercial work, and showerheads must meet tighter efficiency ratings than before. Greywater systems also get clearer code definitions, making it easier to reuse water for irrigation.

## Water heater safety requirements tighten up

New installations now require updated seismic strapping to address earthquake risk, expansion tanks in more situations to manage pressure buildup safely, and safer default temperature settings to reduce scalding risk - a change that matters especially in older Seattle homes with outdated [water heaters](/water-heaters/).

## Drainage and stormwater rules protect Puget Sound

Outdoor drains now need to be sized for heavier rainfall events, backwater prevention devices are mandatory in flood-prone homes, and the code encourages permeable surfaces to reduce runoff into the municipal system.

## Eco-friendly plumbing gets easier to install

Homeowners now have a more streamlined path to rainwater harvesting systems, greywater reuse for irrigation and toilet flushing, and high-efficiency circulation systems that cut down on wasted water while waiting for hot water to arrive.

## Licensing oversight expands

Some tasks that used to be permitted as DIY work now require a licensed professional, with stricter inspection requirements around gas lines, [backflow prevention](/water-filtration-systems/), and slab leak repairs to ensure the work meets code.

## What this means for you

Overall, these changes point toward lower water bills, improved safety, better flood protection, and more consistent workmanship across the board.
