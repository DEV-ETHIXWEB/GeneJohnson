Short-cycling is when a [sump pump](/sump-pumps/) turns on and off rapidly instead of running through a normal, complete cycle. It's more than just an annoyance - pumps draw the most electrical current at startup, so a pump that's constantly restarting is putting far more wear on its motor than one that runs a full cycle and rests.

## A Stuck or Failing Float Switch

This is the most common cause we see. The float switch tells the pump when to turn on based on water level, and if it's positioned incorrectly, catches against the pit wall, or has worn electrical contacts, it can trigger the pump repeatedly instead of completing a normal on/off cycle.

## A Failed Check Valve

The check valve is supposed to stop water from flowing back into the pit once it's been pumped out. When it fails, the pumped water immediately drains back down, refilling the pit and forcing the pump to kick back on right away.

## An Undersized Sump Pit

A pit that's too small can't hold much incoming groundwater, especially during a heavy Seattle downpour. Water rises and falls too quickly for the pump to settle into a normal rhythm, so it cycles constantly just trying to keep up.

## An Oversized Pump

It sounds counterintuitive, but a pump that's too powerful for its pit can also cause short-cycling. It empties the pit almost instantly, the float drops right away, and the cycle repeats in rapid succession.

## Discharge Line Problems

A blocked, frozen, or poorly routed discharge line can prevent water from actually leaving the system, letting it flow back toward the pit or the foundation instead of away from the house.

## Electrical Fluctuations

Voltage instability, loose wiring, or a failing outlet can cause momentary power drops that make the pump restart more often than it should.

## An Aging Pump

Once a pump passes the 7- to 10-year mark, internal components naturally degrade, and short-cycling can be one of the first signs that it's nearing the end of its service life.
