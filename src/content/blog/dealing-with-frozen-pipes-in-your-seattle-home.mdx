Seattle winters don't get as brutal as some parts of the country, but it doesn't take an extreme freeze to put pipes at risk - just a cold snap and a few unprotected feet of piping. Because our homes aren't always built with harsh winters in mind, frozen pipes catch a lot of local homeowners off guard. Here's how to prevent them, and how to tell if you're already dealing with one.

## Preventing Frozen Pipes

**Insulate exposed pipes.** Any pipe running through an attic, basement, crawl space, or exterior wall is vulnerable to freezing temperatures. Foam pipe insulation from any hardware store is inexpensive and easy to wrap around exposed sections before winter sets in.

**Keep the thermostat up.** During a cold snap, keep your household thermostat above 55 degrees at all times, even if you're away. It's a small cost compared to the damage a burst pipe can cause.

**Let faucets drip.** When temperatures drop below freezing, letting a faucet run in a slow drip keeps water moving through the pipe, which helps prevent ice from forming and blocking the line completely. It feels wasteful, but it's a lot cheaper than a repair.

## Signs You Already Have a Frozen Pipe

**No water at all.** If a fixture produces nothing when you turn it on during freezing weather, ice has likely blocked the line completely.

**Odors from the drain.** A frozen section further down the line can cause wastewater to back up, which often shows up first as an unpleasant smell rather than a visible clog.

**Visible frost on a pipe.** Frost forming on the outside of an exposed pipe is a clear sign that freezing is underway or has already happened.

## What to Do Next

If you suspect a frozen pipe, call a plumber right away rather than waiting to see if it thaws on its own - a pipe that's frozen solid is under pressure and can crack or burst as it expands, turning a preventable problem into a flooded basement.
