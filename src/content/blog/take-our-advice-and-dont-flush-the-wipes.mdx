It's a simple habit that causes an outsized number of plumbing calls: flushing wipes instead of throwing them in the trash. We get it — it feels more convenient, and the packaging on some products even calls them "flushable." But our experience on Seattle-area service calls tells a different story.

## Why Wipes Clog Drains

Toilet paper is engineered to break apart quickly once it's wet, which is exactly what lets it move safely through your pipes. Wipes are built for the opposite job. They're made from sturdy, fibrous material designed to hold together while you scrub a surface, and that same durability means they stay intact long after they leave your bathroom. Instead of breaking down, they snag on rough spots inside the pipe, catch other debris, and grow into a blockage.

## The "Flushable" Label Is Misleading

Some manufacturers market their wipes as flushable, and technically they can make it through a toilet and into your home's pipes. But that's not the same as being safe for the sewer system as a whole. Municipal systems across the country, including here in the Seattle area, report ongoing problems with wipes accumulating in pump stations and treatment infrastructure, regardless of what the label says.

## Older Sewer Systems Weren't Built for This

Most city sewer systems were designed decades ago to handle water, human waste, and biodegradable toilet paper — that's it. They were never built to accommodate a durable, non-biodegradable product, and retrofitting that infrastructure to handle wipes at scale is enormously expensive. In the meantime, homeowners are the ones who feel it first, often with an unclogging bill of $300 or more, and in worse cases, damage to a sump or ejector pump that has to work against a wad of wipes.

## Our Recommendation

Keep a small trash can in every bathroom and use it for wipes, no exceptions. It's a small habit that saves you a plumbing bill and helps protect the shared sewer infrastructure everyone in the neighborhood relies on.
